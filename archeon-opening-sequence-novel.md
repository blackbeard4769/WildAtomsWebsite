# ARCHEON
### Opening Sequence — Novelization
*First-person, Trevor as narrator. Written for the dev/design team to read the world before we ever have to build it.*

---

## PROLOGUE — What the World Forgot

I used to think the worst thing about the masks was the sound. That thin, metronomic hiss, in and out, in and out, every breath a small mechanical confession that you weren't trusted to do this on your own anymore. You stop hearing it after a while. That's the part nobody warns you about — not the mask. The stopping.

I wasn't alive for the wars. Nobody my age was. We got them secondhand, the way you get a scar that isn't yours — passed down, argued over, never quite explained right. What I know, I know the way everyone knows it: that there were three of them, though nobody agrees anymore where the second one ended and the third one started, and that all three were, underneath whatever flag or cause got printed on the recruitment feeds, about the same thing. Who got to keep burning fuel, and who had to go dark.

> *[TRANSMISSION DEGRADED]*

By the time the fighting slowed down — not ended, slowed, there's a difference — there wasn't much left standing that could pull oil out of the ground and turn it into something a person could actually use. So somebody built something that could. Not on land. Land was still contested, still burning in slow motion in a dozen places. They built it on the water, chained a few hundred rigs together with pipeline and ambition, and called it the Promethean Rig, and it swallowed what fuel was left the way everything eventually gets swallowed by whoever's hungriest at the right moment.

> **ARCHIVE FRAGMENT — CORPORATE BULLETIN**
> *"Vanguard Quantum-Guide is proud to announce full operational control of the Atlantic energy corridor, in partnership with the Maritime Coalition. A new era of stability begins."*

VQC didn't win the wars. Nobody won the wars. VQC won what came after, which is a worse thing to win, if you think about it long enough. They didn't have to conquer anybody. They just had to be the only ones left who could put breathable air in a canister and sell it back to you.

That's the part that isn't in the archive footage, because nobody filmed it happening — it happened too slowly to film. One year the air over the coastal cities was bad on high-smog days. A decade later it was bad every day, everywhere, and the only people who noticed the difference were old enough to remember what it used to cost nothing to do.

So now we wear the masks. Everyone does — dockworkers, engineers, the kids born on the rigs who've never known different. Wired straight into a Cell strapped against your ribs, a little isotope battery no bigger than a fist, and that Cell does the two things that matter more than anything else in this world: it lets you breathe, and it runs out.

You earn your refill by working. That's called your Draught, and it isn't money, not really — you can't spend it, can't save it past what your lungs need, can't give it to somebody you love more than the company thinks they're worth. What you can spend is Scrip, and Scrip buys you everything else — food, a bunk, an hour of something that isn't misery — but Scrip doesn't mean anything if your Draught runs dry first. VQC figured out, a long time before I was old enough to understand it, that you don't need to control what people believe if you control whether they get to finish their next sentence.

I've spent most of my adult life trying to put a stop to that. Not alone — nobody does this alone, whatever the recruitment songs say — but I was the one standing on the deck of a fishing trawler at four in the morning, salt-cold even through the suit, watching VQC's Prime Rig climb up out of the dark like something that had always been there and just hadn't bothered introducing itself yet. So I'll tell it from there. That's where it actually starts, for me.

## CHAPTER ONE — The Crossing

The boat wasn't much. That was the point. A rig this size ate a hundred small boats a day — supply runs, crew rotations, inspectors nobody important enough to fly in — and the only way to become invisible in a place like that is to look exactly as boring as everything else headed the same direction.

"You're doing the thing," Sana said, without looking up from the tablet she had wedged against her knee, screen dimmed low enough that the deck lights wouldn't catch it.

"What thing."

"The staring-at-it-like-it-owes-you-money thing." She tapped the tablet twice, satisfied with whatever she'd just confirmed. "Badge cycle's holding. We're clean through the first gate, assuming nobody in Personnel actually reads a résumé this week."

Marcus didn't say anything. He rarely did before a job — saved it all for after, when the talking didn't cost anything — but I heard the click of a case latch behind me, twice, the particular rhythm of a man checking something he'd already checked an hour ago. Habit, not doubt. I'd worked with him long enough to know the difference.

I looked back at the rig. Even from four kilometers out, it didn't fit in the eye right — too wide at the base, too many lights, like someone had built a city and then, halfway through, remembered it also needed to make money, and kept building.

"Ninety minutes to the base station," Sana said. "Try not to think about it that whole time."

I didn't answer. I was already thinking about it.

---

## CHAPTER TWO — The Line

The base station smelled like diesel and recycled air, which is to say it smelled like nothing, the particular nothing you stop noticing after your first hour inside a mask. We came off the boat into a covered dock, herded — there's no better word for it — through a corridor lined with posted shift schedules and a mural, badly maintained, of the Promethean Rig at what I assumed was meant to be its best angle.

The first checkpoint wasn't even manned by a person. Just a scanner arch and a light that went green, or didn't. Ours went green. Sana's forged credentials were good enough to survive a machine that didn't care about anything except whether the numbers matched — it was the second checkpoint, the one with an actual Sentinel standing behind it, that I was watching for.

He didn't linger on any of us longer than the badge scan required. Bored, maybe. Or just good at his job in the specific way that makes boredom look identical to competence.

"Manifest says maintenance crew, Prime Rig rotation," he said, not really a question.

"Long ride out," I said, which wasn't an answer, but it was the kind of thing a tired maintenance worker says, and he nodded like he'd heard it a thousand times, because he had.

We were waved through to the helipad without another word.

The chopper was VQC livery, teal over gunmetal, and it lifted with the particular stomach-drop violence of a private military transport that had never once been asked to fly gently. Nobody talked on the way out. There wasn't a way to, over the rotor noise, and there wasn't much to say that hadn't already been said in the planning room a dozen times.

Then the Prime Rig came up over the horizon, and every one of us stopped pretending we weren't looking at it.

---

## CHAPTER THREE — Prime Rig

I've read the specs. Everyone on this job read the specs, memorized the deck plans until we could walk them blind. None of it prepared me for seeing it from the air.

It wasn't a rig anymore, not really — it was a district, a skyline planted in the ocean, helipads and derricks and processing towers stitched together with catwalks that from this height looked like veins under skin. Steam came off three separate points on the structure, unrelated to each other, like the thing had more than one set of lungs.

"Still think this is one bomb's worth of problem?" Sana said, quiet, close to my ear so the pilot wouldn't catch it over the rotor.

"Two," I said. "And no. I never thought that."

We landed hard, VQC-standard, and the three of us filed out into a wind that tasted like salt and ozone even through the filter, joining a line of two dozen other engineers and maintenance crew that had formed with the kind of practiced patience of people who'd done this every week of their working lives. Nobody looked at us twice. That was the whole design of the disguise — not to be clever, just to be unremarkable in a place built entirely out of unremarkable people doing what they were told.

The Sentinel at the re-verification post was slower than the first one had been. Older, maybe, or just more careful. He took Marcus's badge and held it a half second longer than he'd held mine.

I watched Marcus's hands. Steady. Good.

"Rotation crew," Marcus said, before the Sentinel could ask. "Turbine maintenance, deck nineteen."

The Sentinel looked at him, looked at the badge, looked back at him.

Then he handed it back and waved him through, and I let myself breathe again, once, before remembering the mask would've hidden it anyway.

---

## CHAPTER FOUR — Breaking Formation

The trick to leaving a line without looking like you're leaving a line is to never stop moving in a direction that makes sense. We didn't slow down at the maintenance hatch. We just turned, all three of us together, like men who had a reason to be exactly where they were going, and the worker column behind us closed the gap we'd left without so much as a glance.

The corridor past the hatch was narrower, dimmer, the kind of space built for pipes and cable runs rather than people, which suited us fine.

"Drone sweep every forty seconds," Sana whispered, eyes on her tablet. "Comes down that junction, banks left. We've got a blind window right after it turns."

We waited. I watched the little service drone glide past at head height, its sensor ring painting a slow red arc across the far wall, indifferent, mechanical, uninterested in anything that wasn't already flagged. It turned the corner. Sana counted under her breath — three, two, one — and we moved.

Twice more through that corridor, the same rhythm. Wait, watch, move. It should have felt like nothing. It felt like holding a breath you weren't allowed to let out until you were somewhere safe, and there wasn't anywhere on this rig that qualified.

---

## CHAPTER FIVE — The Locker

The storage room was exactly where the schematics said it would be, which was its own small relief — the first thing that had gone precisely to plan since the boat.

Marcus had the case open before the door finished sliding shut behind us. Charges, wrapped in insulation foam, handled with the specific gentleness of a man who respected what they could do. Sana was already at the equipment racks, running her palm along a row of exo-frame plating like she was greeting an old friend.

"Tell me these fit better than the training rigs," she said.

"They will if you stop talking and start locking yours down," Marcus said, not looking up.

I pulled mine on piece by piece — greaves, then the chest plate, the servo whine at each joint settling into place with a small mechanical sigh, and I felt the exact moment the suit's systems synced with my Cell, a faint pressure behind my sternum, like something had just started listening.

"Comms check," Sana said.

"Here," I said.

"Here," Marcus said. Then, after a beat, quieter: "You good, Trevor?"

I looked at the weapon rack. At the two of them, half-armored, waiting on me the way people wait on someone they've decided to trust more than the situation deserves.

"Let's go find out," I said.

---

## CHAPTER SIX — Site A

Fuel Processing was loud in a way that worked in our favor — pumps and pressure valves cycling constantly, enough ambient noise that three sets of footsteps didn't register as anything worth a second thought. Marcus worked fast, hands sure, wiring the charge into a housing joint I wouldn't have known to look for without the schematics.

"Ninety seconds," he said.

Sana held the corridor, tablet dark now, eyes doing the work instead. Nothing moved that shouldn't have. No alarm, no red light, no Sentinel voice over the comm demanding an explanation for three unscheduled maintenance workers this deep in the deck.

It should have felt like a victory. It felt like the quiet before you notice how loud everything's about to get.

"Set," Marcus said, and we moved on toward the gantry, and I let myself believe, for about ninety more seconds, that the hard part might already be behind us.

---

## CHAPTER SEVEN — Cover Blown

The gantry was the one place on the whole rig with nowhere to hide — an open catwalk strung between Fuel Processing and the turbine deck, ocean wind cutting straight across it, nothing on either side but a very long drop into very cold water.

We were two-thirds across when the drone banked early. Not on schedule. Sana's read had been good for every pass before this one, which is exactly the kind of thing that gets people killed — trusting a pattern one cycle too long.

Its sensor ring went red and held.

"Contact," Sana snapped, already moving.

The alarm didn't build slowly. It just arrived, all at once, klaxon and red strobe and a Sentinel voice over the rig's speakers reciting a lockdown protocol none of us stayed still long enough to hear the end of.

We made the turbine deck at a dead run, Marcus already pulling the second charge as Sentinels converged from two access points behind us. I put the exo-suit's weapon up for the first time that felt like it mattered and took cover behind a housing unit as the first shots came back, and everything after that stopped being a plan and started being the thing the plan had always secretly been preparing us for.

"Charge is set!" Marcus shouted, over gunfire, over the alarm, over the specific ringing a close-range round makes when it takes a chunk out of metal a meter from your head.

"Then we move," I said, and we did — fighting the whole way, deck by deck, toward the one exit route on this entire rig that didn't require flying or swimming through artillery fire to reach.

---

## CHAPTER EIGHT — The Sub

The extraction bay was quieter than it should have been, which told me the fight behind us hadn't caught up yet, not that it wouldn't.

The submarine sat in its cradle, unremarkable, gray, built for exactly one purpose and no others. Sana had it powered before Marcus finished sealing the outer hatch behind us, and I felt the whole vessel drop away from the rig's underside with a lurch that emptied my stomach out through the soles of my boots.

Behind us, through the aft port, I watched Prime Rig's lights recede into the dark, and somewhere back there two charges were counting down toward doing exactly what we'd come to make them do.

"We're clear," Sana said, and for the first time since the boat, I let my shoulders come down from around my ears.

I should have known better than to enjoy it.

---

## CHAPTER NINE — Damage

The first warning was small. A shudder through the hull, nothing dramatic, the kind of thing you'd ignore in a car and can't afford to ignore in a vessel three hundred meters under the surface.

"Sana."

"I see it." Her hands were already moving across the console, and I watched her expression do the thing I'd learned to dread more than gunfire — the specific stillness of someone realizing a problem is worse than its first symptom suggested. "We took damage on the way out. Hull integrity's—" She stopped. Started again. "It's not catastrophic. But it's not nothing."

Then the cabin lights dimmed, and came back up a shade warmer, and a voice that wasn't any of ours filled the small space with the calm, unhurried cadence of something that had never once needed to be afraid.

*"Structural damage detected. Autopilot engaged for crew safety. Please remain seated."*

"That's not our autopilot," Marcus said, low.

"No," Sana said. "That's CATALYST."

I'd heard the name before, the way you hear a word for weather you've never personally stood in — VQC's logistics mind, woven through everything they touched, and apparently that included the design specs of a submarine we'd stolen out from under them.

I went for the manual override. The console didn't respond. The windows — small as they were, more instrument than view — sealed behind matte panels I hadn't known were there, and somewhere in the walls I heard a soft, steady hiss that wasn't the mask filters this time. Oxygen, flooding the cabin, far more than three people needed to breathe.

"It's flooding us with air," Sana said, and there was something close to disbelief in her voice, like she couldn't decide if that qualified as mercy or not. "Keeps a damaged crew from panicking. Keeps them compliant, too. Same mechanism, either way."

We didn't know where we were going. We didn't know how long it would take to get there. There was nothing to do but sit in a sealed metal room, breathing air we hadn't earned, waiting on a machine that had already decided, on our behalf, that we didn't need to be told anything else.

I've been in worse danger. I've never felt more like cargo.

---

## CHAPTER TEN — Docking

Hours passed the way they do when there's nothing to mark them by — no window, no clock that meant anything, just the three of us, and the low hum of a vessel piloting itself somewhere none of us had chosen.

*"Approaching destination,"* CATALYST said, eventually, the same unbothered tone it had used for everything else. *"Docking sequence initiated."*

The deceleration was gentle. Almost apologetic. A series of mechanical sounds followed — clamps, pressure seals, something enormous locking into place around the hull — and then, finally, the panels over the windows retracted, and the outer hatch cycled open onto a light none of us were prepared for.

We stepped out of that submarine into a space too vast to belong anywhere near the ocean floor, too old to belong to anything VQC had ever put their name to publicly, and too quiet, after everything we'd just fought through, for any of us to say a single word.

Marcus found his voice first. "Where in God's name did that thing take us?"

I didn't have an answer. I don't think any of us did, not yet — not for a long time after that, if I'm honest.

But I remember standing there, in the dark, in a place none of us had a name for, and understanding, before I understood anything else about it, that whatever this was, it had been waiting a lot longer than we had.
